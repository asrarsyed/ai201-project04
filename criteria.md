# Acceptance criteria - Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next week costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

## Criterions

1. **Criterion**: Across three trials, no more than 1 out of 10 human-written samples may be labelled high-confidence AI.

**Why this target**: I make false positives the strictest target because wrongly accusing a real writer is more harmful than missing some AI-generated text.

--- 

2. **Criterion**: Using the same 10 human samples from Criterion 1, plus 5 AI samples generated by pasting one prompt into a chatbot, the average combined score of the AI set exceeds the average combined score of the human set by at least 30 points, in every trial.

**Why this target**: Reuses the set already needed for Criterion 1 instead of a second hand-authored calibration set, and one AI-generation pass covers the 5 AI samples. 30 points is wide enough that the gap has to come from real separation between the two classes, not noise from a single signal or a lucky sample.

---

3. **Criterion**: Across the 15 total samples from Criterion 2, all three labels — high-confidence human, unsure, and high-confidence AI — are each produced at least once across the three trials.

**Why this target**: No new text needed beyond what Criterion 2 already collects. If one of the three labels never appears on a normal mix of human and AI writing, the thresholds have a dead band that ordinary use will hit, not just an edge case.

---

4. **Criterion**: 100% of valid appeals must change the item's status from its previous status and create a recorded appeal entry containing the decision.

**Why this target**: An appeal that does not actually change status or leave a record is not a reliable appeal process, and users need a clear audit trail.

--- 

5. **Criterion**: For 100% of requests with missing, empty, or oversized text, the service must return a clear 4xx validation response and must not run the detector.

**Why this target**: Rejecting bad input before invoking the model prevents wasted computation and makes failures predictable for whoever is using the service.
